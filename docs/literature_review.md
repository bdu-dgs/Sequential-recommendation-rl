# Literature Review: Support-Aware Offline-to-Online RL for Sequential Recommendation

*Project:* Support-Aware Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning for Sequential Recommendation.
*Compiled:* 2026-10-24. Every paper cited below was checked against its arXiv, proceedings or publisher page. Bracketed numbers point to the reference list in Section 6. R1 and R2 are the two direction-setting web pages, which are not publications.

---

## 1. Scope and how references are used

**Research question.** Historical recommendation logs cover some (state, item) pairs poorly. How can a policy learned offline avoid over-optimism about unsupported items, and when does a limited budget of simulated online interaction improve the policy and when does it degrade it?

**What the two reference pages do.** They set the direction only.
- **R1, Fahmid Morshed Fahid's homepage.** It describes a 2022 Meta PhD ML internship on the People You May Know team. During it he optimized pending friend-request "bidding" in the Facebook news feed with deep RL and contextual bandits, using a model called *Bid And Rank Together* (BART). The page reports a 2% DAU gain in online evaluation. It has no paper and no method details. We use it only as evidence that RL and contextual bandits are used together for ranking in industrial social recommenders. We do **not** reproduce, approximate or claim anything about Meta's internal method.
- **R2, the MIG lab publications page (Chongjie Zhang, WashU).** It shows the lab's lines of work on offline-to-online (O2O) RL and offline policy adaptation. We cite three of those papers as conceptual anchors: BOORL [40], IDAQ [46] and CSP [47]. We do **not** reproduce any of the lab's papers.

**What everything else does.** The remaining citations are technical prior art. They serve three purposes: (i) choosing and correctly implementing baselines, (ii) bounding our novelty claim, and (iii) setting the evaluation protocol.

**Where policy results come from.** All policy-level results come from a clearly labeled simulated sequential environment built from KuaiRec [65]. Its offline logs are generated by a behavior policy whose propensities we know.

## 2. Area syntheses

### 2.1 Discrete offline RL

**The core problem.** Fixed-batch Q-learning fails because of *extrapolation error*: Bellman backups query values of actions that never appear in the data [3]. On Atari data from a single, partially trained behavior policy, Fujimoto et al. found that many off-policy methods, including REM and QR-DQN, did worse than both online DQN and the behavior policy itself [2]. REM's success depended on the large, diverse DQN-Replay dataset [4]. Our logs come from a single, possibly narrow behavior policy, which puts them in the regime of [2], not the regime of [4].

**Value-penalty methods.** Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) [1], in its discrete CQL(H) form, minimizes

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim D}\Big[\log\sum_{a}\exp Q_\theta(s,a)-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim D(\cdot|s)}Q_\theta(s,a)\Big]+\mathcal{L}_{TD}(\theta),$$

where the target is the Double-DQN target $y=r+\gamma\,Q_{\bar\theta}(s',\arg\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a'))$.

- **TD loss.** In d3rlpy's `DiscreteCQL` [22] (default $\alpha=1.0$, DoubleDQN base), $\mathcal{L}_{TD}$ is a **Huber** loss on $Q_\theta(s,a)-y$, not $\tfrac12$ squared error. The original Atari code used QR-DQN distributional targets instead [1]. We report which TD loss we use.
- **Penalty gradient.** The gradient of the penalty with respect to $Q(s,\cdot)$ is $\alpha(\mathrm{softmax}(Q(s,\cdot))-\mathbf{1}_{a_D})$. It pushes down whichever items currently score high, supported or not, and pushes up the logged item.
- **Scale with catalog size.** $\mathrm{logsumexp}_a Q$ lies in $[\max_a Q,\ \max_a Q+\log|A|]$. The penalty therefore grows with $\log|A|$, and a tuned $\alpha$ does not transfer across candidate-set sizes.

**Policy-constraint methods.**

*Discrete BCQ* [2] trains a behavior-cloning network $G_\omega(a|s)$ by cross-entropy and restricts the greedy action to items with sufficient *relative* behavior probability:

$$\pi(s)=\arg\max_{a:\;G_\omega(a|s)/\max_{a'}G_\omega(a'|s)>\tau} Q_\theta(s,a),\qquad y=r+\gamma\,Q_{\bar\theta}(s',\pi(s')).$$

- The TD loss is Huber. The default is $\tau=0.3$ in both the paper and d3rlpy, and d3rlpy adds an imitator logit regularizer with $\beta=0.5$ [22].
- $\tau=0$ recovers (Double) DQN. As $\tau\to1$, only the mode of $G_\omega$ passes the filter, so the policy becomes greedy imitation, not stochastic BC.
- The filter measures relative probability, not sample count. With a near-deterministic $\pi_\beta$ it collapses BCQ to BC. With a diffuse $\pi_\beta$ over thousands of items it admits many items, each of which is almost never observed.

*Other constraint methods.*
- BRAC [9] adds a divergence to $\pi_\beta$. Because we know $\pi_\beta$, the KL term is exact for a categorical policy.
- Fisher-BRC [10] parameterizes $Q=\log\pi_\beta+\text{offset}$. That parameterization transfers to discrete actions and is attractive when $\pi_\beta$ is known. Its action-gradient (Fisher) penalty is continuous-only.

**In-sample methods** never query unlogged items.
- *IQL* [5] fits $V(s)$ by expectile regression ($\tau\approx0.7$–$0.9$) on $Q_{\bar\theta}(s,a)-V(s)$, regresses $Q(s,a)$ onto $r+\gamma V(s')$, and extracts a softmax policy by $\max\ \mathbb{E}_D[\exp(\beta(Q-V))\log\pi(a|s)]$. TorchRL ships `DiscreteIQLLoss`.
- AWR [8], CRR [6] and AWAC [7] use the same advantage-weighted extraction.
- Cost: policy improvement is capped by support, because these methods cannot put mass on good items that were never logged.

**Uncertainty methods.**
- REM [4] provides a multi-head Q-ensemble.
- SAC-N/EDAC [11] take the minimum over a large critic ensemble. SAC-N ports to discrete SAC. EDAC's diversity loss uses action gradients and is continuous-only.
- PBRL [12] penalizes bootstrapped targets by ensemble standard deviation and adds an OOD-action term. In a discrete action space the OOD actions can be enumerated exactly, so this term is cheap for us.

**Excluded or optional methods.**
- TD3+BC [13] is continuous-only; its deterministic actor and MSE-to-action term have no direct discrete form.
- The Decision Transformer [14] is optional. It cannot stitch trajectories, and its return conditioning is unreliable under a stochastic simulated user.
- On when offline RL is worth it, Kumar et al. [15] find that it beats BC mainly with sparse rewards, long horizons and noisy or suboptimal data. *Our inference, not a result stated in [15]:* when behavior data are narrow and near-deterministic, the expected gain of CQL over BC is small. We therefore make behavior-policy temperature a primary experimental axis.

**Large action spaces.**
- Methods for huge or combinatorial action spaces target scales far beyond ours: Wolpertinger's approximate nearest-neighbor selection [16], factored Q [18], BraVE [19] and SPIN [20] address $10^4$–$10^6$+ actions or combinatorial actions.
- At 500–3000 items a full Q-head or softmax is exact and cheap.
- The real problem is *statistical*. Per-item support is sparse, and embedding-shared heads ($Q=f(s)^\top e_a$) spread optimism to unlogged items that resemble logged ones.
- Recsys prior work addresses this through pseudometric action representations [17] and through VPQ's ensemble-variance-weighted Q targets for offline sequential recommendation [21].

**Implications for our design.**
- Discrete CQL is the main offline RL baseline, and its scalar $\alpha$ is exactly what our method generalizes.
- Discrete BCQ is mandatory because it already masks low-support items. Since $\pi_\beta$ is known, its $G_\omega$ can be swapped for the true propensity, which gives an oracle-support variant.
- Discrete IQL is the in-sample alternative.
- REM serves as a non-conservative control and as a source of uncertainty estimates.
- Implementations: d3rlpy [22], with CORL [23] as the O2O protocol reference. CORL itself ships no discrete algorithms and points to Katakomba [24] for discrete control.

### 2.2 Offline-to-online fine-tuning

**Causes of the initial drop.** The literature names four interacting causes.

1. **Value miscalibration inherited from pessimism.** CQL pushes OOD values down by an arbitrary amount. Once online data arrive, backed-up values of exploratory actions can exceed the artificially low values of the pretrained policy's actions, and the policy "unlearns" what it learned offline. Cal-QL [26] fixes this by bounding the push-down from below at a reference policy's value, a one-line change to CQL. SO2 [33] blames biased Q-estimates and wrong Q-rankings, and OPT [42] blames inaccurate offline value estimates and adds a freshly trained critic.
2. **State-action distribution shift.** New online pairs bootstrap from unfamiliar actions [25, 36].
3. **Replay composition and update-to-data (UTD) ratio.**
   - Removing offline data abruptly causes Q-divergence at the switch. WSRL [41] replaces retention of offline data with a short warm-up of pretrained-policy rollouts and then fine-tunes purely online.
   - RLPD [28] runs at a high UTD *stably*, using symmetric 50/50 offline/online sampling, LayerNorm in the critic and a 10-member critic ensemble.
   - SUF [45] stabilizes unconstrained fine-tuning by modifying the UTD ratio.
   - Neither RLPD nor SUF reports that high UTD by itself causes collapse.
4. **Switching objectives between phases.** AWAC [7], IQL [5], Uni-O4 [34] and O3F [39] keep one objective across both phases to avoid this.

**Mechanisms that control the drop.**
- *Calibrated conservatism:* Cal-QL [26].
- *Constraints with decaying weight:*
  - PROTO [31] applies a KL penalty toward the previous, Polyak-averaged iterate, with the weight annealed linearly to 0.
  - Adaptive BC [38] adjusts the BC weight from return feedback.
- *State-adaptive balance:* FamO2O [35] learns a per-state improvement/constraint coefficient.
- *Pessimism by data source:* APL [37] is pessimistic on offline samples and optimistic on online samples.
- *Ensembles:*
  - Lee et al. [25] combine a pessimistic Q-ensemble with balanced replay based on a learned density ratio.
  - ENOTO [32] switches **once**, at the start of online fine-tuning, from a MinQ target to a WeightedMinPair target and adds ensemble exploration.
  - BOORL [40] uses probability matching over a bootstrapped posterior and comes with guarantees against sudden drops.
- *Structural preservation:*
  - PEX [27] freezes the offline policy, adds a new learnable policy and chooses between their proposals with a softmax over Q.
  - JSRL [30] uses a guide policy for roll-in.
- *Critic surgery at the switch:* ACA [36] and OPT [42].
- *Hybrid RL from scratch:* Hy-Q [29] proves efficiency for fitted Q-iteration on offline and online data together, under bilinear rank, when the offline data cover a good policy. That coverage assumption is a formal version of what we call "support".

**Three Regimes [43] (ICML 2026 workshop paper).** Li et al. argue that fine-tuning should preserve whichever offline asset is stronger, the pretrained policy or the dataset. Their predictions matched 45 of 63 empirical cases.

**Discrete-action evidence is scarce.**
- *Reincarnating RL* [44] (Atari) reports that fine-tuning CQL-pretrained agents degrades with 1-step returns, and more so with larger CQL coefficients.
- CCVF [58] includes Atari online fine-tuning with confidence-conditioned values.
- DRIFT [48] fine-tunes discrete-flow policies with a candidate-set approximation on macro-action MinAtar (6^K actions) and Jericho, and uses Cal-QL and IQL as discrete O2O baselines.
- Group work on offline policy adaptation keeps online adaptation in-distribution through uncertainty (IDAQ [46]) or worst-case conservatism over data-consistent models (CSP [47]).

**Implications for our design.**
- Keep one objective across phases: discrete CQL continued, with a modulated penalty.
- Use n-step returns.
- Treat everything inherited at the switch as an explicit ablation: actor and critic weights, target network, **Adam state**, and the offline buffer (retain or drop). Optimizer state is almost never ablated in the literature.
- Include Cal-QL, RLPD-style mixing and a WSRL warm-up as controls.
- Measure the dip directly. Do not assume it.

### 2.3 Support- and uncertainty-aware conservatism

**Support versus density.** BEAR [49] argued that the actor should be constrained to the *support* of $\pi_\beta$ (through MMD), not to match its density. Discrete BCQ [2] is the hard, per-state, discrete form of the same idea. On the bandit side, Sachdeva et al. [97] show that off-policy learning based on inverse propensity scoring (IPS) fails under deficient support, and that restricting the policy space is the most effective remedy.

**Support-weighted penalties.**
- SPOT [50] regularizes the actor with a VAE estimate of $\log\pi_\beta$. During fine-tuning its weight decays linearly and stops at 20% at step 0.8M, and the VAE stays frozen.
- SVR [51] penalizes Q over the whole OOD set, estimated with importance sampling under $\pi_\beta$.
- MCQ [52] trains OOD actions toward pseudo-targets equal to the maximum Q over supported actions, and transfers better to online fine-tuning than CQL.
- Count-MORL [56] uses count-based penalties in model-based offline RL.
- ACL-QL [59] already learns per-(s,a) CQL weights offline, from transition quality.
- SCQ [60] penalizes only OOD actions that are hard to extrapolate. Its point is that raw counts can over-penalize low-support items that lie near well-supported ones in embedding space.

**Uncertainty-weighted penalties and pseudo-counts.**
- UWAC [53] down-weights per-sample losses by MC-dropout variance.
- Anti-exploration [54] and SAC-RND [55] subtract novelty bonuses, from VAE and RND errors respectively.
- MOPO [57] penalizes model rollouts by the maximum, over ensemble members, of each model's predicted standard deviation.
- PBRL's linear-MDP analysis [12] ties ensemble standard deviation to an elliptical LCB term that behaves like an inverse count. That is the bridge between "support" and "uncertainty", and our simulator can test it empirically.

**The closest O2O prior art.**
- **SUNG** [61] keeps a VAE density model updated online. Each minibatch, it marks the top-p% most uncertain samples with a *binary* OOD identifier, possibly chosen by categorical sampling. Those samples get CQL or TD3+BC (SPOT on AntMaze), and the rest get the online objective.
- **DARE** [63] exchanges samples between the constrained and unconstrained objectives, sample by sample, based on deviation from a diffusion behavior model's reference action.
- Evaluation-time switching [62] falls back to a BC agent in uncertain states and extends to O2O.
- CCVF [58] picks a confidence level from the observation history through Bellman consistency. It is discrete-action and includes Atari online fine-tuning.
- In recommendation, **DORL** [64] (KuaiRec) shows that MOPO-style uniform pessimism amplifies the Matthew effect. It relaxes pessimism with a state-level term $P_E=-\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\beta(\cdot|s)\,\|\,\mathrm{uniform})$, estimated from subsequence counts, and keeps the ensemble variance penalty $P_U$. DORL is offline and model-based.

**Implications.** Each building block already exists:
- per-sample weighting [53, 61];
- support-derived penalties [50, 51];
- per-(s,a) $\alpha$ [59];
- relaxation during O2O [61, 63].

What remains for us is the *setting*:
- an exact or oracle $\pi_\beta$ over discrete items;
- a soft weight inside an exact logsumexp;
- relaxation driven by online counts;
- ablations against true support and the true Q, which D4RL cannot provide.

### 2.4 RL for recommendation and simulators

**Datasets.**
- *KuaiRec* [65] has a nearly fully observed small matrix (1,411 users × 3,327 videos; about 99.6% dense, by our arithmetic from DORL's table) and a sparse big matrix.
- *KuaiRand* [66] contains randomly exposed items with 12 feedback signals. It is a near-uniform-logging resource for a second support regime.

**How KuaiRec environments are built.** The table compares the four main conventions.

| Work | User model / reward | Termination | Horizon / repeats |
|---|---|---|---|
| CIRS "KuaiEnv" [68] | DeepFM pretrained on the big matrix, evaluated on the small matrix. Reward is watch ratio, discounted by overexposure $r=y/(1+e_t)$ | Quit if more than $n_Q$ of the last $N$ items share an attribute with the current item ($N=1$, $n_Q=1$) | Max 100 (30 in the limited-rounds setting) |
| DORL [64] | Ensemble of Gaussian DeepFM models on the big matrix. Reward is normalized viewing time (`is_click` on KuaiRand-Pure). Evaluated on the small matrix | Quit if more than $M$ same-category items appear in the last $N$ rounds ($M=0$, $N=4$) | Max 30, no repeats. 100 evaluation trajectories per epoch over 200 epochs |
| EasyRL4Rec [69] | DeepFM user model, with MF imputation for offline evaluation | Three modes: FreeB (repeats, quit), NX_0 (no repeats, quit), NX_X (fixed X rounds, no quit) | Max 30 |
| KuaiSim [67] | Multi-behavior simulator fit to KuaiRand-Pure | "Temper" budget, initialized to the max session length, decreases by the immediate reward each step; the user leaves at a threshold. Plus a retention module | Session and cross-session |

**Evidence that simulators get exploited.**
- EasyRL4Rec [69] reports *preference overestimation*: user models trained with few negatives predict high rewards when true rewards are low.
- Virtual-Taobao [74] needed an action-norm constraint to stop policies over-fitting its GAN/imitation-learned simulator.
- DORL [64] shows that uncertainty pessimism concentrates recommendations on popular categories.
- Sim2Rec [84] trains across an ensemble of simulators.
- S2R-Rec [83] treats the gap between simulator and reality as a dynamics shift.

**Validating and reporting simulator results.**
- KuaiSim validates mainly by held-out click AUC (0.7234 against 0.6866 for the best rebuilt baseline), plus depth and reward comparisons and a geometric fit of return times. It reports no calibration and no error stratified by support.
- Winecoff et al. [85] call for experimental reporting standards in simulation research.
- RecSim [71], RecSim NG [72] and RecoGym [73] are configurable or synthetic controls. RL4RS [70] argues for building evaluation environments from data disjoint from training.

**Methodological warnings.**
- Lee et al. [81] found that on public review benchmarks a greedy immediate-reward recommender beats RL, and that weighting long-term reward more *hurts*. A greedy baseline and evidence of real long-term structure are therefore mandatory.
- Deffayet et al. [82] show that next-item metrics such as HR and NDCG hide offline RL failures.

**Recsys RL methods.**
- Top-K off-policy REINFORCE [76] learns a propensity head. Our known $\pi_\beta$ removes the need for one.
- SQN/SAC [77] attach a Q-head to a cross-entropy sequential recommender, so the cross-entropy head acts as an implicit BC constraint.
- PRL [78] and CDT4Rec [79] are return-conditioned.
- SlateQ [75] covers slates. We deliberately avoid slates.
- The survey by Chen et al. [80] covers the area and lists distribution shift and evaluation as open problems.

**Implications for our design.**
- Because we *generate* the logs with a known $\pi_\beta$, the main experiments can roll out logging and online interaction directly in a ground-truth environment: small-matrix watch_ratio plus a hand-written termination rule. That removes the learned-user-model exploitation channel.
- A DORL/EasyRL4Rec-style setting, with a learned user model trained from big-matrix data and scored on the small matrix, becomes a secondary robustness setting. In it we report the gap between predicted and true return explicitly.
- Sequential structure comes only from the termination and boredom rules, so sensitivity to $(M,N)$ must be reported.
- watch_ratio can exceed 1, so the clipping rule must be stated.
- About 0.4% of small-matrix entries are missing and need a stated mask or imputation.

### 2.5 Contextual bandits and off-policy evaluation

**Bandits.**
- LinUCB [86] chooses $\arg\max_a\ \hat\theta^\top x_{s,a}+\alpha\sqrt{x_{s,a}^\top A^{-1}x_{s,a}}$.
- Riquelme et al. [88] found that many Bayesian deep networks give poor uncertainty in sequential decision-making, while NeuralLinear (Bayesian linear regression on a learned last layer) is robust. This is a direct warning for our uncertainty-gated variant: calibration has to be *checked against true support and error*, not assumed.
- NeuralUCB [89] and NeuralTS [90] need a gradient per candidate item, about 3000 per state, so they are optional.

**Off-policy evaluation (OPE).**
- *Propensity-based estimators:* IPS, SNIPS [91] (the self-normalized $\sum wr/\sum w$), DR [92], sequential DR [93] and replay [87]. They require **known propensities** and **coverage**: $\pi_e(a|s)>0\Rightarrow\pi_b(a|s)>0$.
- *Replay* is unbiased only under uniform logging and keeps about $1/|A|$ of events, which makes it useless at our scale.
- *DR* on actions with zero support collapses to the reward model's extrapolation.
- *FQE* [94] needs no propensities but relies on realizability and concentrability, so it extrapolates on low-support pairs.
- COBS [95] recommends FQE under severe mismatch in stochastic environments, IH for long horizons with mild mismatch, PDWIS for short horizons with mild mismatch, and MAGIC under model misspecification.
- OBP [96] provides tested estimator implementations and a protocol for evaluating OPE by relative error.

**Implications.**
- Real KuaiRec logs have no propensities, so we run **no** propensity-based OPE on them.
- OPE is an *analysis tool* on simulated logs, scored against on-policy ground truth. It is never the primary evaluation.
- Report the following for every estimator: Kish ESS $=(\sum w)^2/\sum w^2$, maximum and p99 weight, mean weight (about 1 under full support), clipping rate, and *unsupported mass* $\mathbb{E}_s\sum_a\pi_e(a|s)\mathbf{1}[\pi_b(a|s)<\epsilon]$. Then plot OPE error against unsupported mass.

### 2.6 Sequential backbones and leakage-free evaluation

**Backbones.**
- SASRec [100] is a causal Transformer. Its causal masking matches the RL state definition (history up to $t$).
- BERT4Rec's [101] apparent advantage comes largely from its full-softmax cross-entropy loss. SASRec trained with full cross-entropy beats it in quality and speed [102].
- BERT4Rec's bidirectional Cloze training does not match the causal RL state.
- GRU4Rec [98] is a cheap alternative encoder. Caser [99] is cited for completeness.

**Evaluation.**
- *Splits.* Splits that ignore the global timeline leak future interactions and change model rankings [103, 104, 107]. A global temporal split is required, and popularity must be computed only from data before the cutoff [104].
- *Metrics.* Sampled metrics are inconsistent with exact ones [106]. With at most 3000 candidates, exact full-ranking metrics are cheap.
- *Rigor.* Sun et al.'s [105] checklist applies: equal tuning budgets for every baseline, explicit split and sampling choices, and multiple seeds.

**Implications.**
- Split the big matrix globally by time ($T_{val}<T_{test}$). Fit the vocabulary, popularity and normalization on pre-cutoff data only.
- Check for interaction-level overlap between the big and small matrices, and exclude small-matrix ground-truth pairs from ranker training.
- Report full-ranking HR, Recall, NDCG@{10,20} and MRR as *secondary* metrics. Simulated return is the primary policy metric.

## 3. Algorithm selection rationale

| Role | Choice | Justification under our conditions (single-item discrete action, 500–3000 items, simulated logs with known propensities) |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised ranker / state encoder | **SASRec with full-softmax CE** over the candidate set; GRU4Rec as the ablation | Causal state matches the RL state. Full CE is cheap at this catalog size and beats BERT4Rec [102]. One encoder is shared by the ranker, BC (the same softmax), the bandit and the Q-heads, so differences between methods come from the decision rule, not the representation. |
| Behavior cloning | Softmax head on SASRec trained by CE | Doubles as $\hat\pi_\beta$ for the "estimated support" arm and as discrete BCQ's $G_\omega$. |
| Main discrete offline RL | **Discrete CQL(H)** with a Double-DQN target, Huber TD loss and n-step returns (d3rlpy defaults as the reference) | Exact logsumexp at $O(|A|)$ per state. Most-cited conservative method with official discrete code [1, 22]. Its scalar $\alpha$ is the object our method replaces with $\alpha\,w(s,a)$, so "CQL with uniform $\alpha$" is the cleanest control. Tune $\alpha$ per catalog size because the penalty scales with $\log|A|$. |
| Mandatory second offline RL | **Discrete BCQ** ($\tau$ sweep, including $\tau=0.3$) in two variants: learned $G_\omega$ and oracle $\pi_\beta$ | Already masks low-support items [2], so it is the main novelty boundary. The oracle-$\pi_\beta$ variant comes free in our simulator. |
| In-sample alternative | **Discrete IQL** (expectile $V$, softmax AWR extraction) | Never queries unlogged items. Stable O2O [5]. Serves as the backbone to try if CQL's underestimation makes fine-tuning fail. |
| Non-conservative control | REM (multi-head) | Expected to be over-optimistic under narrow logs [2, 4]. Its heads also provide a free ensemble-disagreement signal. |
| Online RL / fine-tuner | **Double DQN with n-step returns, LayerNorm critic and a mixed replay buffer**, initialized from the offline critic. The fine-tuned variants continue the CQL objective with a modulated $\alpha$. | Keeps one value-based objective across phases (avoids the switch problem [34, 39]). LayerNorm plus symmetric mixing is RLPD's [28] stability recipe. n-step returns follow the 1-step degradation reported in [44]. Hy-Q [29] gives theoretical grounding for DQN with an offline buffer. PPO is not chosen, because an on-policy objective would force an objective switch from a Q-based offline phase (Uni-O4 [34] shows how heavy keeping PPO in both phases is). |
| O2O controls | Cal-QL, RLPD-style 50/50 sampling, WSRL warm-up with no retained data, ENOTO-style one-time relaxation (optional), PROTO/SPOT-style global decay | These span calibration [26], replay [28, 41], ensembles [32] and time-only adaptivity [31, 50]. |
| Contextual bandit | **Linear-Gaussian bandit on frozen SASRec features** $x_{s,a}=h_s\odot e_a$ (one shared $d\times d$ design matrix), with a **LinUCB** action rule as the primary and **NeuralLinear-TS** as the secondary; plus a **greedy one-step** model ($\alpha=0$) | LinUCB is canonical [86]. NeuralLinear is the most robust deep-bandit posterior [88]. A shared parameterization avoids having 3000 disjoint models. The greedy baseline is required by Lee et al. [81]. NeuralUCB/NeuralTS [89, 90] are too costly per candidate. |
| Recsys-specific baselines | Popularity (pre-cutoff), SQN [77], DORL [64] (secondary, in the learned-simulator setting) | Standard in the KuaiRec RL literature [64, 69]. |

## 4. Novelty check for the support-aware fine-tuning method

| Candidate design | Closest prior works | What is actually different in our setting | Risk of being a rename |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Support-weighted conservatism**: CQL push-down weighted per (s,a) by a support signal, applied during fine-tuning | SUNG [61] (VAE density, binary top-p% OOD identifier, CQL during O2O); SPOT [50] (global decay, frozen VAE); SVR [51]; ACL-QL [59] (learned per-(s,a) $\alpha$, offline); Count-MORL [56]; DARE [63]; MCQ [52]; DORL [64] (state-level entropy relaxation, offline) | Exact and *oracle* $\pi_\beta$ over discrete items. A soft, continuous weight inside an exact logsumexp, instead of a binary identifier or hard exchange. Weights relaxed by online counts. Recsys outcomes (long tail, category domination). Counterfactual controls (shuffled or inverted support) that need ground truth. | **Medium–high.** Algorithmically it is roughly "discrete, soft SUNG". Defensible only as a controlled design study. |
| **(b) Ensemble-uncertainty-gated updates** | VPQ [21] (recsys, offline); PBRL [12]; EDAC [11]; UWAC [53]; Lee et al. [25]; ENOTO [32] (one-time switch); BOORL [40]; evaluation-time switching [62]; CCVF [58] | The only new element is an empirical test of whether disagreement tracks true support (AUROC for detecting $\pi_\beta<\epsilon$), plus per-action gradual relaxation. | **High.** VPQ + ENOTO + BOORL cover most of it. |
| **(c) Constraining policy shift on low-support actions** (item-level KL trust region weighted by 1/support, or a BCQ mask relaxed by online counts) | Discrete BCQ [2]; BEAR [49]; Sachdeva et al. [97]; BRAC [9]; PROTO [31]; FamO2O [35]; adaptive BC [38]; Virtual-Taobao ANC [74] | Per-item rather than per-state or global weighting. Exact KL with known $\pi_\beta$. | **High.** Reads as "discrete BCQ with an online-updated mask" or "support-weighted PROTO". |
| **(d) Dynamic offline/online replay mixing** | Balanced replay [25] (learned density ratio); RLPD [28]; APL [37]; WSRL [41]; SUF [45]; Hy-Q [29] | Replaces the learned density ratio with the known propensity. | **Very high.** RLPD and WSRL are strong controls, and "support-weighted sampling" alone is not new. |

**Recommendation: design (a), framed as a controlled design study.** The claim is not a new algorithm. The question is: *in discrete-item O2O fine-tuning, does a per-(s,a) support signal matter for conservatism beyond (i) any adaptivity and (ii) simply less conservatism?* Designs (b), RND [55] and counts enter as alternative *estimators* of the same quantity. SUNG [61], SPOT [50], SVR [51], ACL-QL [59], DARE [63] and DORL [64] are cited as direct antecedents, and CCVF [58] as discrete-action O2O prior art.

**Instantiation (support-weighted CQL, SW-CQL).** The objective is

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a_B)\sim\mathcal{B}}\Big[\log\sum_a w_t(s,a)\,e^{Q_\theta(s,a)}-Q_\theta(s,a_B)\Big]+\mathcal{L}_{TD}(\theta).$$

The weight is

$$w_t(s,a)\propto\big(c_t(s,a)+c_0\big)^{-\kappa},\qquad c_t(s,a)=M\,\pi_\beta(a|s)+n^{on}_t(\bar s,a).$$

- **Pseudo-count.** $c_t$ combines oracle (or BC-estimated $\hat\pi_\beta$) logged support at scale $M$ with online visit counts $n^{on}_t$ per (state cluster $\bar s$, item).
- **Normalization.** $w_t$ is normalized so that $\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mathrm{softmax}Q}[w]=1$, which matches the strength of uniform CQL.
- **Gradient.** The penalty gradient is $\alpha(\mu_w(a|s)-\mathbf{1}_{a_B})$ with $\mu_w\propto w\,e^{Q}$. Low-support items therefore get pushed down more.
- **Special cases.** $\kappa=0$ recovers CQL(H) exactly.
- **Cal-QL compatibility.** Cal-QL's lower bound $\max(Q,V^{\pi_\beta}(s))$, with $V^{\pi_\beta}$ from logged Monte-Carlo returns, can be layered on, which isolates calibration from support weighting.

**Hypotheses.**
- **H1.** At matched mean conservatism, SW-CQL beats uniform CQL and Cal-QL in narrow-behavior regimes (low behavior temperature or heavy popularity skew). It shows a smaller initial dip and lower Q-overestimation on low-support items, and the advantage shrinks as behavior coverage approaches uniform.
- **H2.** The gain depends on the signal itself. Shuffled support should perform like uniform CQL, inverted support should be worse, and oracle support should be at least as good as estimated support, with the estimated-support arm degrading as $\hat\pi_\beta$ quality falls.
- **H3.** With small online budgets, uniform-CQL fine-tuning can end below its offline starting point. Support weighting lowers the budget at which fine-tuning becomes net-positive.

**Minimal ablations.**
1. *Matched strength.* Compare against uniform $\alpha$ set to the same mean weighted strength, **and** against full $\alpha$-sweep frontiers, giving the uniform baseline its best $\alpha$.
2. *Shuffled support.* Permute $w$ across pairs, preserving its marginal distribution.
3. *Inverted support.* Penalize high-support items more.
4. *Signal source.* Oracle $\pi_\beta$; $\hat\pi_\beta$ at several BC qualities; exact counts; RND; ensemble std. Report each signal's rank correlation with oracle support.
5. *Schedule.* Frozen $w$ vs. online-count-relaxed $w$ vs. SPOT/PROTO-style global time decay vs. Cal-QL.
6. *Hard vs. soft.* SUNG-style binary top-p%, APL-style gating by data origin, BCQ mask.
7. *Inheritance at the switch.* Optimizer state, target network, offline buffer (retain or drop), with WSRL warm-up as a control.

**Experimental axes.** Behavior temperature, $\epsilon$ and popularity skew (data coverage); online budget; catalog size (500 / 1500 / 3000).

**Metrics.**
- Dip depth.
- Final return and area under the fine-tuning curve.
- $\hat Q-Q^{true}$, stratified by support bin.
- Policy mass on low-support items.
- Coverage and long-tail exposure.
- Majority-category domination (MCD) [64].

**Statistics.** At least 10 seeds, reported as IQM with bootstrap confidence intervals.

## 5. What our project does NOT claim

- We do not reproduce, approximate or reverse-engineer Meta's BART or any internal Meta system (R1). We do not reproduce the MIG lab's papers (R2).
- We do not claim a new RL algorithm. SW-CQL is an instantiation and controlled analysis of an idea space already occupied by [50, 51, 59, 61, 63, 64].
- We make no claim about real-user engagement, retention or online A/B outcomes. All policy numbers are *simulated return in a named environment version*, with its reward definition, termination rule, horizon and candidate set stated.
- We do not claim simulated gains transfer to Kuaishou. The ground truth is a static watch_ratio matrix with no context effects, and long-horizon structure comes from hand-written termination and exposure rules.
- We do not run propensity-based OPE on real KuaiRec logs, because they have no propensities. Oracle-support results rely on a privilege that real systems lack. The estimated-$\hat\pi_\beta$ arm is the realistic one.
- We do not claim state-of-the-art next-item accuracy. Ranking metrics are secondary sanity checks.
- We provide no theory. Hypotheses are tested empirically.
- We address single-item actions only, not slates [75].
